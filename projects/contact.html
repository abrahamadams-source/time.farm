“Contact,” 2024–

“Contact” is a series of views through antique encyclopedia pages held up to light so that front and back form chance combinations of text and image.

Foreword

“Contact”

David Joselit

As a noun, contact denotes diverse forms of interpersonal encounter across different scales—a clash of civilizations (first contact), an erotic adventure (sexual contact), physical tournaments (contact sports), or someone’s personal information stored in your phone. Each type of contact constitutes a social configuration with its own choreography and power relations. In photography, as in other mechanical procedures, contact has a technical meaning: a contact print is one whose image appears by placing a negative on a photo-sensitive surface and then applying light in the controlled setting of a darkroom. As a verb, contact performs the imperative to engage (“Contact me immediately,” says every text ever). The photographs in Abraham Adams’ Contact rearticulate these three dimensions of contact—the physical, the technical, and the imperative—in enactments of connection across difference and loss.

Adams’ photographs emerge from a chain of physical touch: each print is made from a section cut from an Encyclopedia Britannica owned by the artist’s father who died in 1988 while moving his books. The touch of the father is reanimated by the touch of the son suggesting an oedipal drama. The cut-outs follow the outlines of diagrams on one side of pages with unrelated texts on the other. These sheets are then mounted on a window and photographed with a Macro lens. The daylight shining into Adams’ “lightroom” studio creates a kind of contact print in which both sides of the thin paper, one representing a diagram and the other a text, appear superimposed upon one another in the resulting image (where the verso is seen in reverse). In short, through contact with the sun (homophonic with the son), the readymade paper negative brings two faces of a single page into graphic contact with one another. There is a poignancy in any sheet of printed paper—the page is a single material, but what is printed on either side can never touch, never be seen at once. Adams’ photographs stage an imperative to make contact across this conjuncture of sameness and difference—the Janus face of the printed page. In the end this is not an oedipal but an anti-oedipal form of photography: the sameness and difference represented in the relation between father and son, who are joined by shared genetic and family heritage, but who like the sheet of paper, have two distinct faces, two distinct relations to language, are not submitted to an oedipal contest for domination as Freudian theory would indicate. Instead, they are held in fraternal suspension as a chimerical hybrid.

Knowing the source of the photographs in Contact enriches the work, but its anti-oedipal dimension is legible without the backstory. In later theorizations of the Oedipus complex, flowing from the Freudian revision of Jacques Lacan, oedipal authority is aligned with linguistic authority—what Lacan called “The Name of the Father.” In Adams’ photographs the authority of language is disseminated through its contamination by diagrammatic representations (as well as the cutting of semantic units and the reversal of letters). Text is radically spatialized, returned to the sources of alphabetic signs as images. These photographs are poems of a very specific kind because their poetics articulates the diagrammatic and the syntagmatic together as they are embedded in the skin of the page, whose half-tone “pigmentation” and pulpy blemishes are perfectly visible in the prints. Knowledge is a form of tattooing here, which rhymes with Adams’ method of cutting. It is a cutting without castration; a form of loss—of language (and of the father)—that need not result in submission to the law. An anti-oedipal photography.
